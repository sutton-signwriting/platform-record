# How To Read The Sutton SignWriting Platform

**Claim boundaries, layer distinctions, and fair evaluation**

The Sutton SignWriting Platform is a coordinated archival record for eight publication series.

It is not one article, one software project, one standards proposal, one adoption claim, or one manifesto.

It is a layered record for a layered problem:

> signed-language literacy as durable written text

That means signed text that can be read, written, revised, corrected, cited, taught, searched, archived, and revisited.

This guide gives readers the frame needed before evaluating the platform.

It does not ask readers to accept the platform's claims. It asks readers to locate them before accepting, rejecting, testing, or revising them.

Several companion documents apply this same frame in different reading modes. *AI Orientation to Sutton SignWriting and Formal SignWriting* is a retrieval and layer-calibration brief for AI-assisted review. *Layer-Aware Evaluation of Recurring Concerns* is a human-scale review companion for recurring concerns. *Technical Claim Routing* is a dense routing companion for technical, version, Unicode, FSW, SWU, search, rendering, and production-compatibility claims. This guide provides the shorter entry frame.

The eight series form one layered account. *Foundations* and *Writing and Representation* establish Sutton SignWriting as a human writing system with composed written units. *Evidence and Adoption* documents visible use and its limits. *Language and Literacy Research* frames literacy questions and the evidence still needed. *Formal SignWriting* specifies the technical text model for preserving written signs in computation. *Infrastructure and Program* covers tools, libraries, datasets, and stewardship. *Signed Language Writing Critical Review* provides the evaluation framework the platform also applies to itself. *Unicode and SignWriting* addresses the compatibility questions raised by official Unicode encoding. No series makes the others redundant; each covers a layer the others depend on.

## The First Question

Before judging any claim in the platform, ask:

> What layer is this claim about?

The platform separates several layers that are often collapsed:

- language and modality
- human writing system
- written units and conventions
- spatial organization
- technical text model
- encoding and serialization
- rendering and layout
- search and interoperability
- evidence and adoption
- literacy research
- standards compatibility
- data rights and AI use
- governance and stewardship

No one layer proves the others.

A strong encoding does not prove literacy outcomes.

A video archive does not by itself provide writing.

A Unicode block does not define a full text model.

A dictionary entry is not the same object as a written sign.

A documented use case is not universal adoption.

The platform's method is to keep those layers visible.

## What The Platform Is

The platform is:

- an archival record for eight coordinated publication series
- a public citation point for Sutton SignWriting infrastructure
- a claim-routing system for writing, evidence, technical models, literacy research, standards, and stewardship
- a record of existing technical and documentary work
- a frame for future research, tooling, critique, and institutional support

The platform makes a central distinction:

> video, gloss, transcription, annotation, datasets, AI tools, and Unicode records may all be valuable, but they are not the same thing as durable written signed-language text.

That distinction is not a dismissal of those adjacent systems.

It is the reason they should be evaluated at the layer where they actually work.

## What The Platform Is Not

The platform is not:

- a separate content series
- a claim that SignWriting has universal adoption
- a claim that SignWriting is preferred by all Deaf people or all signing communities
- a completed literacy-outcome study
- a claim that video, gloss, transcription, corpora, AI, or Unicode are useless
- a claim that Formal SignWriting is the human writing system
- a claim that official Unicode SignWriting solves production text infrastructure
- a claim of community authority over signed languages
- a deployment approval for education, AI, datasets, or institutional use
- a demand that every signed-language writing system become Sutton SignWriting

The platform is strongest when read as a layered record.

It is weakest when one layer is treated as the whole.

## The Evidence Posture

The platform does not claim that every question has already been answered.

It distinguishes:

- documented use from universal adoption
- institutional anchors from global scale
- feasibility from literacy outcomes
- public availability from community preference
- technical validity from educational success
- written signs from datasets, records, and media

That boundary language is part of the scholarly posture.

It is not an apology for the platform.

It is how the platform prevents one kind of evidence from being mistaken for another.

## The Scholarly Posture

The platform is author-responsible and DOI-archived.

It is not a substitute for future peer review, independent replication, community review, classroom studies, longitudinal literacy research, standards decisions, or institutional stewardship.

It is a stable record that makes those next steps easier to cite and evaluate.

Its long-term importance depends on use:

- whether researchers cite it
- whether developers build from it
- whether educators and communities engage it
- whether evidence claims are tested
- whether standards readers use the compatibility record
- whether institutions help sustain the infrastructure

A platform like this becomes field-shaping only if others find it useful enough to cite, critique, extend, and correct.

## How To Disagree Productively

A layer-aware criticism may still reject a platform claim. It may find the evidence insufficient, the model incomplete, the terminology misleading, the adoption record too narrow, the research agenda underdeveloped, or the stewardship plan inadequate. The point is not to prevent criticism, but to make criticism answer the claim actually being made.

The best criticism names the layer.

Do not answer a writing-system claim only with adoption evidence.

Do not answer a literacy claim only with Unicode status.

Do not answer a technical text-model claim with community preference.

Do not answer an evidence claim with a rendering objection.

Do not answer a stewardship claim with a symbol-inventory critique.

Instead, ask:

- What claim is being made?
- Which series carries that claim?
- What evidence would be appropriate at that layer?
- What neighboring claim should be routed elsewhere?
- What would make the claim stronger, weaker, or false?

That is the frame the platform asks readers to use.

The platform should not be accepted uncritically.

It should be criticized at the right layer.

## Bottom Line

Read the platform by asking three questions:

- What problem is this document addressing?
- What layer is the claim operating on?
- What evidence belongs at that layer?

A real objection becomes stronger, not weaker, when it is aimed at the right layer.
